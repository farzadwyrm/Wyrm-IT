# Map one workflow, score each step, cap by risk

Consultants and automation teams do not decide what to automate by intuition. They follow a stable four-move sequence: scope the process (SIPOC), walk through a real instance to get the steps and their metrics (value stream or metrics-based process mapping, recorded in a simplified BPMN-style map, with Lean waste probes, and process or task mining when event data exists), score each candidate on two axes (value or automation potential against ease or feasibility, behind hard disqualifying gates), and then turn the result into a business case of volume × handling time × automation share × loaded labour cost. AI agents add two things that RPA-era methods lack. The first is a capability test: does the step need judgement or unstructured language, which favours an LLM, or is it fixed rules on structured data, which favours plain code? The second is a risk cap: under the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF and vendor agent guidance, a step's money, reversibility, personal-data and people-decision exposure limits how much autonomy it may have. The best combined method for a self-serve SMB tool is **an LLM-run, story-based interview ("think of the last one") that fills a SIPOC boundary plus a metrics-based step table, scores every step on rule-basedness, input form and AI fit, assigns one of four classes, caps that class by a highest-dimension-wins risk tier, and computes hours saved with UiPath's benefit logic (potential % × baseline time including rework), shown as low/base/high scenarios with every assumption labelled by where it came from.** No published, validated rubric exists for this exact job, so the specification at the end is a sourced synthesis. Its thresholds and default rates are design defaults to calibrate against real deployments, not benchmarks.

## Discovery converges on scope, walk-through, measure, then probe for waste

Every serious discovery method starts by fixing the boundary before detail. **SIPOC is a one-page, five-column table (Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers)**, which AHRQ recommends at the start of a project because it is easy to use, needs little or no training and defines scope ([AHRQ](https://digital.ahrq.gov/health-it-tools-and-resources/evaluation-resources/workflow-assessment-health-it-toolkit/all-workflow-tools/sipoc)). University of Illinois guidance keeps the Process column to **about 4–7 high-level steps** so the boundary is agreed before mapping in detail ([U. Illinois AITS](https://www.aits.uillinois.edu/UserFiles/Servers/Server_474/File/Professional_Services/BPI/BPI-SIPOC-HowToGuide.pdf)). SIPOC records no time, volume, decisions or exceptions, so it opens the analysis but cannot finish it.

The measurement layer comes from Lean. The LEI lexicon defines value stream mapping as diagramming every step of material and information flow with cycle time, lead time and **%C/A, the "percentage of product completed with perfect quality"** at each step ([LEI](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/value-stream-mapping/)). For office and service work, Karen Martin and Mike Osterling's metrics-based process mapping (MBPM) puts **process time (PT), lead time (LT) and %C&A on each swimlane activity**. It rolls these up into an activity ratio (PT ÷ LT, which exposes waiting) and rolled first-pass yield (the product of the %C&A values, which exposes rework). Analysis starts by circling the longest lead times, lowest %C&As and highest process times ([Karen Martin Group](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/metrics-based-process-mapping/7123821)). Red Hat's practitioner summary explains why this matters for automation: when lead time far exceeds process time, work is "sitting in queues or waiting on someone else", and this typically happens at handoffs ([Red Hat](https://www.redhat.com/en/blog/metrics-based-process-mapping-mbpm)). These four quantities (hands-on time, elapsed time, right-first-time and who hands to whom) are the ones an SMB owner can estimate without data. That makes MBPM, not manufacturing VSM, the right measurement model.

The map notation needs to be readable, not executable. BPMN 2.0 defines a **Descriptive conformance subclass**, Bruce Silver's "Level 1", whose palette is "largely carried over from traditional flowcharting" ([BPM Institute](https://bpminstitute.org/resources/books/resources-books-bpmn-method-and-style)). It contains none, message and timer start events, user and service tasks, exclusive and parallel gateways, pools and lanes, and data objects and stores ([Visual Paradigm](https://s.visual-paradigm.com/guide/mastering-bpmn-descriptive-modeling-a-practitioners-guide-to-the-essentials/)). That subset is enough to draw an SMB workflow as a swimlane diagram. A timer start marks scheduled work, an XOR gateway is an "if… otherwise…" decision, and a message flow is a handoff to a customer or supplier. Exceptions belong to the richer Analytic subclass. A self-serve tool can collect them as plain text ("what goes wrong here and what do you do then?") without exposing any notation.

Lean waste gives the diagnostic probes. Gemba Academy's office examples of the eight wastes include **transferring files between software programs** (transportation), **waiting for approvals or responses** (waiting), **incorrect report data that must be redone** (defects) and **a talented analyst given only basic administrative tasks** (unused skills) ([Gemba Academy](https://www.gembaacademy.com/gemba-insights/the-seven-or-eight-deadly-wastes)). Re-keying between systems is the strongest automation signal in office work. Waiting at approvals is the strongest signal for a human-check redesign.

Process and task mining replace estimates with evidence, but they are built for enterprises. Process mining needs an event log with **Case ID, Activity and Timestamp**, and optionally a Resource ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-automate/process-mining-processes-and-data); [van der Aalst](https://www.processmining.org/old-version/files/event_logs_the_input_for_process_mining.pdf)). From that log, tools such as Celonis show frequency-ranked variants with throughput times ([Celonis](https://docs.celonis.com/en/variant-explorer.html)). Task mining instead records desktop clicks, keystrokes and screenshots to find repetitive task candidates. Even then, UiPath's own documentation says a business analyst, not the AI, decides automation potential ([UiPath Task Mining](https://docs.uipath.com/task-mining/docs/unassisted-task-minining-introduction)). An SMB has no event log, so the practical substitute is to store the interview result in an event-log-compatible shape (case, activity, resource, estimated duration). It can then be checked later against timestamps exported from the owner's inbox, booking system or accounting tool.

Interview technique determines data quality more than notation does. Practitioners define the happy path "when there are no exceptions, problems, or conditions" first and then "delve into the necessary exceptions" ([Modern Analyst](https://modernanalyst.com/Careers/InterviewQuestions/tabid/128/ID/3626/How-would-you-build-a-Business-Process-Model.aspx)). HEFLO warns that **managers describe the process as designed while operators describe it as executed**, and that exceptions consuming much of the effort surface only through follow-up. Its question set runs from scope and triggers, through "walk through the last instance step by step", to decisions, exceptions, systems and double entry ([HEFLO](https://blog.heflo.com/elicitation-techniques/)). Story-based interviewing in product discovery makes the same point: ask about the last specific occurrence, then "excavate" chronologically because first answers are thin ([Product Talk](https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-story-based-interviewing/)). A web tool cannot observe a gemba walk. Anchoring every question on the last real instance is the closest substitute.

## Suitability scoring is a two-axis funnel guarded by disqualifiers

RPA vendors publish the most transparent scoring logic, and their tools share one architecture. **UiPath Automation Hub's High-Level Assessment asks five 1–5 Likert questions**: how rule-based (very creative = 1 to very rule-based = 5), how digital the input, how structured the input, expected change in the next six months, and documentation (none = 1 to detailed work instructions = 5). The answers are averaged into a 0–100% Idea Score ([UiPath API guide](https://docs.uipath.com/automation-hub/automation-suite/2.2510/api-guide/input-for-high-level-assessment)). Other UiPath documentation splits this into Suitability (the average of the first three factors) and Readiness (the average of the last two), and admits the two descriptions are inconsistent ([UiPath Idea Score](https://docs.uipath.com/automation-hub/automation-cloud/latest/user-guide/information-about-the-idea-score)). The Detailed Assessment then computes a yes/no **Feasibility** from process and application stability. It also computes an **Automation Potential %** that starts at 100% and is reduced by unstructured, non-digital or variable input, and an **Ease of Implementation %** banded Easy 65–100, Medium 35–65 and Difficult 0–35 ([UiPath Detailed Assessment](https://docs.uipath.com/automation-hub/automation-cloud/latest/user-guide/information-about-the-detailed-assessment-algorithm.md)). In older versions, "major change expected" disqualifies an idea outright and sets it On Hold ([UiPath 2021.10](https://docs.uipath.com/automation-hub/automation-suite/2021.10/user-guide/information-about-the-detailed-assessment-algorithm)). UiPath does not publish its internal weights or multipliers.

Blue Prism's free Process Assessment Tool scores the same two axes, Automation Potential and Ease of Implementation, and maps the pair to **Prioritize, Review or Defer** ([Blue Prism PAT](https://documentation.blueprism.com/pat/en-us/user-guide/process-assessment.htm)). Its benefit logic is identical in spirit: potential value equals the value of process time × the percentage of the process automated ([Blue Prism PAT FAQ](https://bpdocs.blueprism.com/pat/en-us/faqs.htm)). Mining-led tools such as Automation Anywhere Discovery Bot and Microsoft Process Advisor substitute observed path frequency and rework for self-reported Likert answers. Neither publishes a rubric ([Automation Anywhere](https://docs.automationanywhere.com/bundle/enterprise-v2019/page/discovery-bot/topics/discovery-bot-opportunities.html); [Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/power-platform/blog/?p=11109)).

The consultancies use the same two axes for gen-AI under different labels. **Gartner's Use-Case Prism plots business value against feasibility**, with quadrants of likely wins, calculated risks, marginal gains and selective exceptions ([Microsoft WWPS summary of Gartner](https://wwps.microsoft.com/?p=12638)). Deloitte's May 2026 matrix crosses user desirability with technical sophistication into **Scale, Enhance, De-prioritize and Stop**, adds a financial-attractiveness overlay, and sets decision gates at 90 days, 6 months and 12 months ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/dk/en/services/consulting/perspectives/closing-the-ai-value-gap.html)). Info-Tech's agentic-AI version adds **risk and data readiness** as explicit criteria next to value and feasibility ([Info-Tech](https://www.infotech.com/research/assess-and-prioritize-agentic-ai-use-cases-in-government)). BCG's "10-20-70" rule, which puts 70% of AI effort in people and processes, explains why consultancies put process discovery ahead of model choice ([BCG](https://www.bcg.com/capabilities/artificial-intelligence)). McKinsey's estimate that current technology could automate activities absorbing **60–70% of employees' time** describes technical potential, not a forecast ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/mgi/our-research/the-economic-potential-of-generative-ai-the-next-productivity-frontier)). It is no basis for a per-workflow saving rate.

The failure literature explains why gates come before weights. RPA "won't fix a process that you don't fully understand, or that is fundamentally broken" ([TechBeacon](https://techbeacon.com/enterprise-it/4-robotic-process-automation-project-fails-avoid?amp)). Overlooked exceptions drive maintenance cost ([UiPath](https://www.uipath.com/blog/rpa/rpa-and-the-roi-conundrum)). Academic selection studies list execution time, stability, complexity, data type and failure rate as core criteria ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-031-46594-9_11)). A systematic review counts 32 criteria in use ([Farinha et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.11951.pdf)), and Leshob et al. note there is no easy automatic method ([ÉTS](https://pure.etsmtl.ca/en/publications/a-rule-based-method-to-effectively-adopt-robotic-process-automati)). The most-cited quantitative selection paper, Wanner et al. at ICIS 2019, is marked **withdrawn** ([AIS eLibrary](https://aisel.aisnet.org/icis2019/business_models/business_models/28)). The honest conclusion is that **the two-axis-plus-gates architecture is industry consensus, but no public, validated weighting exists**. Any tool that shows a numeric score must present its weights as its own.

## AI agents earn a step only where deterministic rules break

The RPA rubric rewards rule-based, structured, digital input. An agent rubric has to reward a different profile. OpenAI's agent guide says agents suit "workflows where traditional deterministic and rule-based approaches fall short". It names three conditions: **complex decision-making with nuanced judgement or exceptions, rules too complex to maintain, and heavy reliance on unstructured data**. It warns that otherwise "a deterministic solution may suffice" ([OpenAI](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)). Anthropic separates workflows, where LLMs and tools follow "predefined code paths", from agents that "dynamically direct their own processes". It advises "the simplest solution possible", noting that agents trade cost and latency for performance and that their errors compound ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). The only controlled comparison in the notes, Průcha et al. (2025), found that **UiPath RPA beat a computer-use agent on speed and reliability for repetitive, stable tasks**, while the agent cut development time and adapted better to interface changes. The authors recommend hybrids, though the study covers only one tool of each kind ([arXiv 2509.04198](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04198v1)).

This inverts part of the RPA logic. A step with structured input and fixed rules belongs to code or an integration platform, not an agent. A step that reads email, documents or speech, or applies written guidelines with judgement, is where an LLM adds value. A step whose output must be exact, such as a sum, a calendar write or an invoice posting, should be executed by code even when an LLM interpreted the input. The architecture that follows, "LLM interprets, code executes, human approves what matters", is what Anthropic calls a workflow with an LLM step. It, rather than a free-roaming agent, fits most SMB processes.

Task-level research supplies the vocabulary for grading a step between full automation and human work. Eloundou et al. rate a task as exposed when an LLM can **cut its time by at least 50% at equal quality**. Their tiers are E0 (no exposure, including physical work), E1 (the LLM alone) and E2 (LLM-powered software) ([arXiv 2303.10130](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.10130v5)). Stanford's WORKBank adds the worker's view with a five-level Human Agency Scale, from **H1 (the AI handles the task entirely) to H5 (continuous human involvement)**. It also adds a "Red Light" zone where AI is capable but workers do not want automation. **Distrust of accuracy was the top concern (45%)** among workers negative about AI ([arXiv 2506.06576](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06576)). The practical lesson for a self-serve tool is to ask the owner which steps they want to keep, as well as what software could do. Autonomy frameworks then translate the class into an operating mode. Feng et al.'s levels run from the user as Operator (L1) to Observer (L5) ([arXiv 2506.12469](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12469v1)). AWS's scoping matrix separates agency from autonomy: in Scope 2, "prescribed agency", a human approves all consequential actions, and in Scope 3 the agent acts under monitoring with kill switches ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/the-agentic-ai-security-scoping-matrix-a-framework-for-securing-autonomous-ai-systems/)).

| Suitability class | Who acts | Autonomy level (Feng) | AWS scope | Typical SMB example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Automate | Code, integration or LLM step with no per-item approval | L5 (monitored) | Scope 3 | Logging an enquiry into the CRM, sending a templated receipt |
| AI agent with human check | Agent proposes or acts; human approves or samples | L3–L4 | Scope 2 | Drafting a quote reply that the owner approves |
| AI-assist | Human acts; AI drafts, summarises, extracts or prepares | L1–L2 | Scope 1 | Summarising a complaint before the owner calls back |
| Keep human | Human only | L1 or none | None | Site visit, sensitive negotiation, hiring decision |

## Risk tier, not capability, sets the ceiling on autonomy

Agent guidance and regulation converge on rating actions by consequence. OpenAI tells builders to rate each tool low, medium or high risk "based on factors like read-only vs. write access, reversibility, required account permissions, and financial impact". It requires human intervention for actions that are **"sensitive, irreversible, or have high stakes"**, such as cancelling orders or issuing large refunds, especially early in deployment ([OpenAI](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)). Anthropic adds checkpoints, maximum-iteration stopping conditions, ground-truth checks at each step and sandboxed testing ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). NIST's Generative AI Profile lists **"Human-AI Configuration" (automation bias and over-reliance)** among its twelve GenAI risks, alongside confabulation, data privacy and information security ([NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)). A human check that has become a rubber stamp is therefore itself a risk. The NIST AI RMF's Govern, Map, Measure and Manage functions give the process frame ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)). ISO/IEC 42001 control A.5 makes AI impact assessment auditable, although only secondary summaries of that paywalled standard were available ([SureCloud](https://www.surecloud.com/resource-hub/iso-42001-annex-controls)).

For most SMB agents, the EU AI Act's binding duty is transparency. **Article 50 requires that people be told they are interacting with an AI system unless that is obvious, at the latest at first interaction** ([AI Act Art. 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). Law-firm summaries of the Digital Omnibus put Article 50 in force from **2 August 2026** and defer Annex III high-risk obligations to **2 December 2027** ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/); [Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)). The final regulation text was not verified on EUR-Lex, so these dates should be rechecked before they appear in product copy. Annex III makes certain SMB-relevant uses high-risk: **recruitment and screening, task allocation based on personal traits, performance monitoring, creditworthiness, and life or health insurance pricing** ([AI Act Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/)). Article 6(3) exempts systems that perform a narrow procedural or preparatory task without materially influencing the decision. Profiling of natural persons is always high-risk, and relying on the exemption must be documented ([AI Act Art. 6](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/6/)). For a self-serve tool, this means a step that decides something about a person's job, credit or insurance can at most get AI preparation, with the human deciding.

These sources yield one design rule. Score each step on money, external exposure, commitments, reversibility, personal-data sensitivity, decisions about people and safety; **the highest dimension sets the tier, and the tier caps the suitability class**. The notes found no source giving accuracy thresholds such as "automate above X% accuracy". Thresholds must come from each deployment's own evaluation, which is why the guardrails below include a supervised ramp-up period.

## Savings estimates are gross capacity, not cash, unless stated otherwise

UiPath's Detailed Assessment is the clearest published benefit model. **As-is time = processing time + rework time from errors + audit or review time.** FTEs equal that time divided by working days × hours per day. **Benefit (hours) = Automation Potential % × total as-is time**, and cost benefit applies the same percentage to annual employee cost ([UiPath Detailed Assessment](https://docs.uipath.com/automation-hub/automation-cloud/latest/user-guide/information-about-the-detailed-assessment-algorithm.md)). It is a gross figure with no term for exception handling or run cost. UiPath's own ROI guidance fills the gap. It builds the case over **24–36 months**, reports pilot time savings of **"typically 30 to 70%"**, uses a fully loaded hourly cost covering benefits, facilities, training and turnover, and expects strong ROI within about 18 months in most cases ([UiPath](https://www.uipath.com/blog/rpa/rpa-and-the-roi-conundrum)). Practitioner sources put employment cost at about **30–40% above wages** and warn against inflated automation rates and ignored exception costs ([Auxiliobits](https://www.auxiliobits.com/?p=20358)).

How the inputs are elicited matters as much as the formula. Answer scales anchor estimates: Schwarz et al. showed respondents infer "normal" from the range of the options offered ([ISR](https://api.isr.umich.edu/?p=190617)). Shorter reference periods inflate reported frequencies ([LMU](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/452/)). Recall of work time use, however, is comparatively accurate ([PMC7745111](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7745111/)). The method should therefore ask for an open number for a concrete recent period ("last week") and confirm whether that week was typical. It should ask minutes for the last item rather than hours per week, and multiply volume by time itself. Two caveats belong in front of the user. Released hours become cash only if headcount, overtime or paid outsourcing actually changes. And the LLM run cost of agents, meaning tokens and human review of AI output, is not modelled in any vendor framework in the notes.

## The recommended method: a conversational mini-PDD with gated, risk-capped scoring

The best combination for a self-serve tool takes one element from each tradition. SIPOC sets the boundary. A story-based "last time" walk-through yields the happy path. MBPM supplies the per-step metrics (hands-on time, wait, right-first-time). Descriptive BPMN draws the map. Lean waste probes catch re-keying and waiting. UiPath's five factors are inferred per step from the walk-through answers rather than asked as abstract Likert items. An added AI-fit factor follows OpenAI's three agent criteria and the 50% exposure test. A WORKBank-style "keep this human" question respects owner preference. A highest-dimension risk tier caps autonomy. UiPath's benefit formula, extended with exception and oversight terms, produces the savings figure. The output document mirrors UiPath's Process Definition Document, which has sections for purpose, applications, as-is map, statistics, detailed steps, to-be scope, and business exceptions kept separate from application errors ([UiPath PDD](https://docs.uipath.com/task-capture/docs/details-about-the-pdd)). It is reduced to a "mini-PDD".

The interview should run as an LLM conversation over a fixed slot schema rather than a static form. Xiao et al. found an AI chatbot survey produced significantly more informative, relevant, specific and clear answers than Qualtrics across about 600 participants. The authors are affiliated with the chatbot vendor ([arXiv 1905.10700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10700v2)). A later LLM interviewer study found better engagement than surveys but less depth than human interviewers ([arXiv 2309.10187](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.10187)). That gap argues for mandatory probes on exceptions and decision rules, the slots LLMs under-probe. Extraction into a strict JSON schema, with nullable fields for unknowns, keeps the record valid without inventing values ([OpenAI Structured Outputs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/structured-outputs)). Length is the binding constraint. SurveyMonkey data shows drop-off rising most steeply over the first 15 questions, and completion falling 5–20% beyond 7–8 minutes ([SurveyMonkey](https://www.surveymonkey.com/curiosity/survey_questions_and_completion_rates/); [SurveyMonkey](https://surveymonkey.com/curiosity/survey_completion_times)). The design therefore targets a **6–7 minute median**. The LLM pre-fills per-step fields from the walk-through, and the owner confirms them on a single step-table card instead of answering each field separately. This follows UiPath's two-tier pattern of an instant score followed by an optional deeper stage ([Auxis on UiPath](https://auxis.com/?p=20905)).

## Implementable specification

Each element below is labelled with its lineage. "Sourced" means the structure comes directly from a cited source. "Synthesis" means it combines cited sources in a way no single source states. "Default" means a value chosen for this tool that must be calibrated, and it is shown to users as an assumption.

### 1. Interview questions in order

The interviewer asks these in order. Questions marked "LLM pre-fill" are drafted from the owner's walk-through (Q6) and shown for confirmation or correction on one card, not asked one by one. Every numeric question offers "rough guess is fine" and "I don't know", and an unknown is stored as `null`. Required questions block the result. Optional questions fall back to stated defaults.

### 3. Scoring rubric and mapping to the four classes

Three per-step factor scores use 1–5 scales modelled on UiPath's Likert anchors. The AI-fit scale is a synthesis of OpenAI's agent criteria and the E0–E2 exposure tiers.

| Score | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rule** (`decision_type`) | `none` | `fixed_rule` | `guided_judgement` | `experience_judgement` | `relational_or_physical` |
| **Input** (digital and structured, `input_format`) | `system_fields` | `structured_form` | `message_text` or `document` | `phone_call` or `paper_photo` | `in_person_physical` |
| **AI fit** (LLM can halve time at equal quality) | Reading, classifying, extracting or drafting from `message_text`/`document` with `none`/`fixed_rule`/`guided_judgement` | `phone_call` handled by voice agent, or `guided_judgement` on digital input | `experience_judgement` on digital input, where drafts or summaries help | `paper_photo` needing OCR plus judgement, or `relational` on digital input | `in_person_physical` (E0) |

Deterministic fit is `det_fit = (Rule + Input) / 2`, in the spirit of UiPath's Suitability average ([UiPath](https://docs.uipath.com/automation-hub/automation-cloud/latest/user-guide/information-about-the-idea-score)). Capability class is assigned by the first matching rule (synthesis). Thresholds are defaults.

| Order | Condition | Capability class |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `already_automated` | Keep as is (shown separately, not scored) |
| 2 | `owner_keep_human` or AI fit = 1 | Keep human (AI-assist offered as an option if AI fit ≥ 2) |
| 3 | `det_fit ≥ 4` and `exception_share ≤ 0.2` and `integration_access ≠ none_physical` | Automate (deterministic: code or integration) |
| 4 | AI fit ≥ 4 and Rule ≥ 3 and `exception_share ≤ 0.3` | AI agent with human check (eligible for Automate if Tier 1) |
| 5 | AI fit ≥ 3 | AI agent with human check if Rule ≥ 3; otherwise AI-assist |
| 6 | AI fit = 2 | AI-assist |
| 7 | Otherwise | Keep human |

Final class = the lower of capability class and the tier cap, ranked Automate > Agent with human check > AI-assist > Keep human. Tier 1 LLM steps from rule 4 are promoted to Automate with sample review. Workflow-level gates override everything. If `change_expected = major`, every step is labelled "Defer: redesign first", following UiPath's disqualifier. If the workflow's rolled first-pass yield (the product of the step first-pass fractions) is below 0.5, the tool flags "fix the process before automating", following the warning that RPA "won't fix a process… fundamentally broken" ([TechBeacon](https://techbeacon.com/enterprise-it/4-robotic-process-automation-project-fails-avoid?amp)).

Priority sits separately from class and follows the Gartner, Blue Prism and Deloitte two-axis logic. The value band uses baseline hours per year for the step: under 10 = 1, 10–50 = 2, 50–150 = 3, 150–400 = 4, over 400 = 5. Feasibility is the mean of three scores. Stability scores 5 for no change, 4 for minor, 2 for some and 1 for major. Integration access scores 5 for API or connector, 3 for email only, 2 for UI only or unknown, and 1 for physical. Exception score = 5 − 4 × `exception_share`. The quadrants are value ≥ 3 and feasibility ≥ 3 = **Do first**, value ≥ 3 and feasibility < 3 = **Plan carefully**, value < 3 and feasibility ≥ 3 = **Quick tidy-up**, and otherwise **Not worth it yet**. These bands are defaults. The structure is sourced from Gartner's likely wins, calculated risks, marginal gains and selective exceptions ([Microsoft WWPS](https://wwps.microsoft.com/?p=12638)) and Blue Prism's Prioritize, Review and Defer ([Blue Prism PAT](https://documentation.blueprism.com/pat/en-us/user-guide/process-assessment.htm)).

### 4. Risk-tier rules and guardrails

The tier is the highest tier any flag triggers. The tier rules are a synthesis of OpenAI's tool-risk factors, AWS scopes and the EU AI Act. The sampling rates and money bands are defaults.

| Tier | Triggered by any of | Class cap | Human-in-the-loop pattern | Guardrails |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 Low** | No flags, or `personal_data` that is internal only and `reversibility = easy` | Automate | Monitor plus post-hoc sample review: 100% of the first 20 outputs, then 5% | Action log; iteration and retry limits; ground-truth checks at each step; kill switch ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents); [AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/the-agentic-ai-security-scoping-matrix-a-framework-for-securing-autonomous-ai-systems/)) |
| **2 Moderate** | `external_message`, routine `personal_data`, `money` under 50, or `commitment` with `reversibility = easy` | Automate for deterministic steps (det_fit ≥ 4); Agent with human check for LLM steps | Confidence or failure-threshold escalation to staff; templates for outbound messages; sample review of 100% for the first 50, then 10% | Article 50 AI disclosure at first contact ([AI Act Art. 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)); scoped, least-privilege tools; output validation against a schema; PII filtering; relevance and prompt-injection screening ([OpenAI](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)) |
| **3 High** | `money` of 50 or more, any refund or payment, `destructive_write`, `commitment` with `reversibility ≠ easy`, `sensitive_data`, or `legal_statement` | Agent with human check, where the check is pre-approval of each action | Agent proposes and a human approves (AWS Scope 2), or deterministic validation plus explicit customer confirmation before commit | Everything in Tier 2, plus an audit trail of approvals; spend caps; no delete permission for the agent; approval UI shows source data and reasoning and requires key fields to be confirmed, against automation bias ([NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)); approval and override rates tracked |
| **4 Restricted** | `decision_about_person` (hiring, performance, pay, task allocation by traits, credit, insurance), `safety`, emergency triage | AI-assist (preparation only) or Keep human | The human makes the decision; AI performs only narrow procedural or preparatory work, in the Art. 6(3) style | Documented assessment of the exemption; no profiling; a note that Annex III obligations apply from 2 Dec 2027 per the Omnibus summaries ([AI Act Art. 6](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/6/); [Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)); recommend professional advice |

Every tier also inherits the NIST Measure and Manage loop: log errors and overrides, and re-score the step after 90 days of live data, as Deloitte's 90-day gate does ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/dk/en/services/consulting/perspectives/closing-the-ai-value-gap.html)). GDPR Article 22, on solely automated decisions with significant effects, was outside the gathered sources. It should be added to Tier 3 and Tier 4 copy after legal review.

### 5. Agent workflow design output

The to-be design is generated deterministically from the final classes, following Anthropic's "simplest solution possible" rule ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). The trigger becomes an event listener (inbox, form, call or schedule). Each Automate step with det_fit ≥ 4 becomes a code or integration action. Each LLM-handled step becomes an LLM call with a named purpose (classify, extract, draft, converse) whose output is validated against a schema before any code acts on it. Each human check becomes an approval or review task placed before the first Tier 3 action. Each AI-assist step becomes a "prepared for you" artifact, such as a draft, summary or checklist. Each exception becomes an explicit escalation route to the named role. The design specifies a single agent unless step order is genuinely unpredictable. Every tool is listed with OpenAI's read/write, reversibility, permission and financial-impact rating. Stopping conditions are a maximum of 3 retries, then escalation (a default). The KPIs to track are handling time, error rate, escalation rate and approval override rate.

### 6. Savings formula, defaults and display

The formula below is a synthesis that extends UiPath's benefit model ([UiPath](https://docs.uipath.com/automation-hub/automation-cloud/latest/user-guide/information-about-the-detailed-assessment-algorithm.md)) with exception and oversight terms.

```
V_yr        = volume_typical_per_week × W
e_i         = 1 − first_pass_out_of_10_i / 10
H0_i        = V_yr × touch_minutes_i × (1 + e_i × k_rw) / 60          # baseline hours/yr, incl. rework
Saved_i     = H0_i × (1 − x_i) × s(final_class_i, scenario)           # exceptions stay human
Saved_wf    = Σ Saved_i − M × W                                         # minus oversight time
Value       = Saved_wf × wage × L                                       # only if wage given
Net/yr      = Value − run_cost_yr ;  Payback_months = build_cost / (Net/yr / 12)   # only if costs entered
Elapsed-time benefit (not monetised) = Σ wait removed at steps reclassified away from human handoffs
```

| Parameter | Default | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| W, working weeks per year | 48 | Default; UiPath uses net working days ([UiPath](https://docs.uipath.com/automation-hub/automation-suite/2.2510/user-guide/completing-the-detailed-assessment)) |
| k_rw, rework time as a share of step time | 0.5 | Default; rework term per UiPath baseline |
| x_i, exception share | Step value, else workflow value from Q14, else 0.2 | Default fallback |
| s, Automate (low / base / high) | 0.60 / 0.80 / 0.90 | Default; above UiPath's 30–70% pilot range because exceptions are already removed |
| s, Agent with human check | 0.30 / 0.50 / 0.70 | UiPath "typically 30 to 70%" ([UiPath](https://www.uipath.com/blog/rpa/rpa-and-the-roi-conundrum)) |
| s, AI-assist | 0.15 / 0.30 / 0.50 | Default; capped at the 50% exposure threshold ([arXiv 2303.10130](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.10130v5)) |
| s, Keep human | 0 | Definition |
| M, oversight hours per week per live workflow | 0.5 (low scenario 1.0, high 0.25) | Default |
| L, loaded-cost multiplier on wage | 1.3 (range 1.3–1.4) | Practitioner range ([Auxiliobits](https://www.auxiliobits.com/?p=20358)) |
| run_cost, build_cost | Not assumed; entered or quoted | No source models LLM run cost |
| Horizon | 1 year shown; 24–36 months for any payback statement | [UiPath](https://www.uipath.com/blog/rpa/rpa-and-the-roi-conundrum) |

The result screen leads with **hours of capacity released per week and per year as a low–base–high range**. Each step's contribution is shown on the process map, so the owner can see which step drives the number. Money appears only if the owner gave a wage, and it carries the label "value of time released, which is not cash saved unless you reduce paid hours, overtime or outsourcing". An assumptions panel lists every input with its provenance badge ("you said", "you estimated", "we inferred, you confirmed", "default"), and each value is editable with a live recalculation. Defaults are visually distinct, and the tool states which single assumption moves the base figure most. Volume, time per item and the saving rates are expressed in the owner's own units ("about 12 enquiries a week × 9 minutes"), echoing the decomposition the owner already confirmed in Q8. Open questions, meaning required slots left `null`, are shown as reasons the range is wide rather than filled with guesses.

## Conclusion

The real gap in existing practice is not missing methods but missing integration. RPA scoring rewards rule-bound, structured steps. Agent guidance rewards the opposite, judgement over unstructured input. Regulation and agent-safety guidance cap both by consequence. A tool that scores only "automatability" will either overrate re-keying steps that a cheap integration could handle or recommend agents for money-moving steps that need a human approver. Treating capability (deterministic fit versus AI fit) and consequence (risk tier) as independent axes, combined by taking the minimum, is the methodological move that makes a per-step "automate / agent with check / assist / keep human" label defensible.

Its weakest points are empirical, and the product should be honest about them. No validated weights, accuracy thresholds or SMB self-estimation error rates exist in the sources. The saving rates are therefore assumptions, and the method's credibility rests on making them visible and editable, then re-scoring after 90 days of real logs. Collecting confirmed interview records alongside post-deployment timestamps would let the tool calibrate its own defaults over time, turning the self-reported mini-PDD into a lightweight form of the process mining that SMBs cannot otherwise afford.
